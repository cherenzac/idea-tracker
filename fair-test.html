What does the exact test measure?

The two-sided exact McNemar test compares the number of A-only and B-only passes using a fair-coin binomial model. Ties contribute to the pass rates, but not to the test. The p-value is not the probability that A and B are equal.


Pairs should be independent of one another. Repeated or related cases, shared failure modes, or testing many metrics need a different design or adjustment. With few discordant pairs, this test can miss real effects. A small p-value alone does not establish a useful improvement. Inputs stay in this page; no upload, storage or tracking.

Will a review catch a rare error?


Compare the chance of finding at least one error in a random review with the same review budget split across two slices. Enter assumed rates, not claims about an actual system.
















What do the results assume?

Each review independently has its slice’s assumed error rate, and reviewers catch every error they see. For n random reviews at error rate p, the chance of detecting any error is 1 − (1 − p)n. The split plan multiplies the two slices’ chances of missing all errors before subtracting from one.


In the fictional example, the targeted slice is 10% of traffic but gets 20% of reviews. To compare plans for the same underlying population, derive the overall error rate using traffic shares, not review shares. Detection odds are not a prevalence estimate or a guarantee. Correlated cases, missing labels, or imperfect review can change the odds. Nothing is uploaded or saved.
